# Radiology Modalities: Key Characteristics and Clinical Applications

This guide provides a comprehensive overview of the six major imaging modalities used in modern radiology, highlighting what each does best, their fundamental principles, strengths, limitations, and contraindications.

## Computed Tomography (CT)

### Basic Principles
- Uses X-rays rotating around the patient to produce high-resolution cross-sectional images[^4]
- Rapid acquisition minimizes motion artifacts
- Often enhanced with intravenous iodinated contrast media[^4]
- Converts X-ray attenuation data into digital grayscale images[^11]

### Strengths
- Excellent bone and soft tissue differentiation[^4][^10]
- Fast imaging suitable for trauma and emergency settings[^4]
- Multiplanar and 3D reconstructions possible[^10]
- Can be used in patients with metallic implants (unlike MRI)[^10]
- Eliminates superimposition of structures outside area of interest[^10]
- High spatial resolution (0.5-1mm)[^10][^11]

### Limitations
- Involves ionizing radiation; higher dose than standard X-rays[^4]
- Contrast media risks include nephrotoxicity and allergic reactions[^4]
- Limited use in pregnant patients due to radiation risks[^4]
- Cannot distinguish between tissues of similar density without contrast[^4]

### Contraindications
- Pregnancy (especially first trimester)[^4]
- Known allergy to iodinated contrast media (for contrast studies)[^4]
- Severe renal impairment without appropriate precautions[^4]
- Weight exceeding scanner table limits[^4]

## Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI)

### Basic Principles
- Uses strong magnetic fields and radiofrequency pulses to image hydrogen nuclei[^5]
- Produces high-contrast soft tissue images without ionizing radiation[^5]
- Multiple sequences (T1, T2, FLAIR, diffusion) highlight different tissue properties

### Strengths
- Superior soft tissue contrast[^5]
- Multiple imaging sequences enable tissue characterization
- No radiation exposure[^5]
- Excellent for neurological, musculoskeletal, and pelvic imaging
- Functional imaging capabilities (perfusion, spectroscopy, fMRI)[^5]

### Limitations
- Longer scan times; sensitive to patient movement[^5]
- Claustrophobia and noise may affect patient tolerance[^5]
- High cost and limited availability[^5]
- Low sensitivity (10^-3 to 10^-5 mol/L)[^5]
- Small population difference between nuclear spin states limits signal[^5]

### Contraindications
- Implanted metallic devices (pacemakers, cochlear implants)[^5]
- Ferromagnetic foreign bodies, especially in eyes[^5]
- Some gadolinium-based contrast agents contraindicated in severe renal failure[^5]
- Claustrophobic patients may require sedation[^5]

## Ultrasound (US)

### Basic Principles
- Uses high-frequency sound waves reflected by tissues to create real-time images[^6]
- Doppler ultrasound assesses blood flow direction and velocity[^6]
- Non-ionizing imaging modality[^6]

### Strengths
- No ionizing radiation; safe in pregnancy and pediatrics[^6]
- Portable and relatively inexpensive compared to other modalities[^6]
- Real-time dynamic imaging useful for guiding interventions[^6]
- Can view moving structures such as heart valves and tendons[^6]
- Excellent for superficial structures, abdominal organs, and fetal assessment[^6]

### Limitations
- Operator-dependent; requires significant expertise[^6]
- Limited penetration in obese patients or through bone/air[^6]
- Lower resolution compared to CT/MRI for deep structures[^6]
- Cannot visualize structures behind bone or air-filled organs[^6]

### Contraindications
- None significant; generally safe for all patient populations[^6]
- Some limitations with specific transducer types and clinical questions[^6]

## Nuclear Medicine (NM)

### Basic Principles
- Uses radioactive tracers emitting gamma rays to assess physiological function[^7]
- Images represent functional rather than detailed anatomical information
- Combines with CT/MRI for anatomic correlation (PET/CT, SPECT/CT)[^7]

### Strengths
- Provides metabolic and functional data rather than just anatomy
- Excellent for oncology, cardiology, endocrinology applications[^7]
- Can detect disease before anatomical changes occur[^7]
- Useful for whole-body assessment in single examination[^7]

### Limitations
- Lower spatial resolution than CT or MRI
- Radiation exposure from radiotracers[^7]
- Limited anatomical detail; often combined with CT or MRI
- Typically used as second-line investigation in many scenarios[^7]

### Contraindications
- Pregnancy and breastfeeding (relative contraindications)[^7]
- Allergies to radiopharmaceuticals (rare)
- Recent administration of competing radiopharmaceuticals[^7]
- Inability to remain still for acquisition time[^7]

## Fluoroscopy

### Basic Principles
- Real-time X-ray imaging producing continuous moving images[^8]
- Uses image intensifiers or flat-panel detectors[^8]
- Often combined with contrast agents to visualize hollow organs and vessels[^8]

### Strengths
- Dynamic assessment of organ function (e.g., GI motility, joint movement)[^8]
- Guides interventional procedures with real-time feedback[^8]
- Minimally invasive approach for many diagnostic studies[^8]
- Visualizes moving structures that static imaging might miss[^8]

### Limitations
- Radiation exposure, potentially prolonged during interventions[^8]
- Requires contrast agents with risk of allergic reactions[^8]
- Lower resolution than standard radiographs for some applications[^8]
- Operator dependent for image acquisition and interpretation[^8]

### Contraindications
- Pregnancy (relative; minimize exposure)[^8]
- Contrast allergies (for contrast-enhanced studies)[^8]
- Unstable patients who cannot maintain required positioning[^8]

## Mammography

### Basic Principles
- Low-dose X-ray imaging specialized for breast tissue[^9]
- Detects differences in tissue density and microcalcifications[^9]
- Advanced techniques include digital breast tomosynthesis (3D mammography)[^9]

### Strengths
- Effective for breast cancer screening and diagnosis[^9]
- Tomosynthesis reduces tissue overlap, improving detection[^9]
- Contrast-enhanced mammography provides physiological information[^9]
- Superior for detecting microcalcifications compared to other modalities[^9]
- Reduces breast cancer mortality through early detection[^9]

### Limitations
- Radiation exposure, though substantially lower than CT[^9]
- Reduced sensitivity in dense breasts[^9]
- Cancers may appear similar to normal glandular tissue[^9]
- Patient discomfort during compression[^9]

### Contraindications
- Allergy to contrast media (for contrast-enhanced studies)[^9]
- Inability to tolerate breast compression or positioning[^9]
- Pregnancy (relative contraindication)[^9]
- Recently placed breast implants (timing-dependent)[^9]
