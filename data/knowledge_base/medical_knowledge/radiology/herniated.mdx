# Radiology Knowledge: Herniated Bladder - Comprehensive Guide

## Definition and Classification

- Bladder herniation occurs when a portion of the urinary bladder protrudes through a defect in the abdominal wall, most commonly into the inguinal canal (IBH - Inguinal Bladder Hernia).
- Classification based on relationship to peritoneum:
  - **Paraperitoneal**: Most common (60%), bladder herniates alongside peritoneal sac
  - **Extraperitoneal**: Bladder alone herniates without peritoneal involvement
  - **Intraperitoneal**: Bladder herniates within peritoneal sac (least common)
- Additional classification by location:
  - Inguinal (most common)
  - Femoral
  - Obturator
  - Perineal
  - Sciatic

## Epidemiology and Risk Factors

- Incidence: 1-3% of all inguinal hernias, 10% in patients >50 years
- Demographics: Predominantly older males (50-70 years)
- Risk factors:
  - Age >50 years (weakened pelvic musculature)
  - Obesity (BMI >30)
  - Lower urinary tract obstruction (BPH, urethral stricture)
  - Previous pelvic surgery or trauma
  - Chronic increased intra-abdominal pressure (COPD, chronic constipation)
  - History of previous herniorrhaphy (recurrent hernia)

## Pathophysiology

- Combination of factors:
  - Weakening of pelvic floor musculature with age
  - Decreased bladder tone from outlet obstruction
  - Adherence of perivesical fat to hernia sac
  - Loss of bladder fixation to anterior abdominal wall
  - Chronic increased intra-abdominal pressure
- In paraperitoneal type (most common), peritoneum creates traction on bladder wall during hernia formation

## Clinical Presentation

- Often asymptomatic (30-40% discovered incidentally)
- Characteristic symptoms when present:
  - **Two-stage micturition** (pathognomonic): Initial spontaneous void followed by manual compression of hernia to complete emptying
  - **Mery's sign**: Reduction in hernia size after voiding
  - Inguinal or scrotal swelling that changes with bladder filling
- Associated urinary symptoms:
  - Frequency, urgency, nocturia
  - Dysuria
  - Recurrent UTIs
  - Hematuria (in cases with strangulation)
- Complications may present as acute urinary retention, renal failure, or scrotal pain

## Imaging Modalities and Findings

### Retrograde Cystography (Gold Standard)
- Wide-mouthed, rounded protrusion of bladder wall extending into hernia sac
- Enhanced visualization during voiding phase due to increased intravesical pressure
- Technical considerations:
  - Patient positioning critical (erect films detect 100% versus 30% in supine position)
  - Oblique projections helpful to distinguish from midline cystocele
  - Post-void films essential to detect filling defects

### Computed Tomography (CT)
- Primary diagnostic study for surgical planning and complication assessment
- Characteristic findings:
  - Angulation of bladder base anteroinferiorly toward hernia side
  - Direct visualization of bladder tissue within hernia sac
  - Assessment of hernia contents (bladder alone or with bowel/omentum)
  - Evaluation for complications (hydronephrosis, strangulation)
- Technical protocol:
  - Multiplanar reconstructions (axial, sagittal, coronal) essential
  - Contrast-enhanced studies improve tissue definition
  - Prone positioning may enhance contrast filling of herniated portion
  - Pre- and post-void imaging offers functional assessment

### Ultrasound
- Limited utility, primarily for large scrotal hernias
- Sonographic findings:
  - Fluid-filled sac continuous with urinary bladder
  - "Beaked" appearance at cranial portion entering inguinal canal
  - Dynamic assessment shows size change and wall thickening after micturition
  - Color Doppler evaluates vascularity and potential complications
- Advantages: Non-invasive, no radiation, point-of-care availability

### MRI
- Provides detailed anatomical assessment for complex cases
- Findings parallel CT but with superior soft tissue contrast
- Special value:
  - Relationship of hernia to inferior epigastric vessels (direct vs. indirect classification)
  - Assessment of bladder wall integrity and potential neoplastic changes
  - Evaluation of adjacent pelvic organ involvement

### Intravenous Urography (IVU) - Historical Significance
- Indirect signs:
  - Small asymmetric bladder
  - Incomplete visualization of bladder base
  - Lateral displacement of distal ureter
  - Only 30% detection rate in supine position

## Differential Diagnosis

- Bowel-containing inguinal hernia
- Hydrocele
- Spermatocele
- Epididymal cyst
- Varicocele
- Testicular tumor
- Scrotal abscess or hematoma
- Inguinal lymphadenopathy
- Undescended testis

## Preoperative Considerations

- Imaging recommendations for high-risk patients:
  - Males >50 years with inguinal swelling and LUTS
  - Obese patients (BMI >30)
  - Patients with history of BPH
  - Previous herniorrhaphy
  - Diagnosis primarily confirmed by CT or retrograde cystography
- Preoperative urological assessment to:
  - Evaluate for bladder outlet obstruction
  - Rule out underlying malignancy if hematuria present
  - Assess baseline renal function

## Surgical Management

### Operative Approach
- Open repair with mesh (Lichtenstein technique most common)
- Laparoscopic approaches (transabdominal preperitoneal or totally extraperitoneal)
- Two-stage repair may be considered for complex cases:
  - Initial laparoscopic reduction
  - Delayed definitive repair

### Specific Techniques
- Gentle dissection of hernia sac with identification of bladder component
- Reduction rather than resection whenever possible
- Mesh placement with care to avoid direct bladder contact
- Intraoperative cystoscopy to:
  - Guide dissection in complex cases
  - Identify inadvertent bladder injuries
  - Confirm complete bladder reduction
- Catheter drainage for 24-72 hours postoperatively

### Indications for Bladder Resection
- Necrotic or gangrenous bladder wall
- Bladder diverticulum with narrow neck
- Presence of bladder wall tumor
- Irreducible herniation

## Complications

### Perioperative
- Bladder injury during herniorrhaphy (12% risk without preoperative diagnosis)
- Urinary extravasation
- Mesh erosion into bladder
- Wound infection (higher risk if bladder opened)

### Late Complications
- Urinary tract infection
- Hernia recurrence
- Bladder outlet obstruction
- Hydronephrosis from ureteral involvement
- Vesicoureteral reflux
- Urolithiasis in chronically herniated bladder portion

## Postoperative Management

- Catheter management:
  - Simple reduction: 24-48 hours
  - Bladder injury repair: 7-14 days with cystogram before removal
- Follow-up imaging:
  - Post-void residual assessment at 2 weeks
  - Repeat cystography if persistent symptoms
- Voiding trial before catheter removal
- Urodynamic studies if persistent LUTS

## Prognosis and Outcomes

- Excellent outcomes with preoperative diagnosis and planned repair
- Most patients experience significant improvement in urinary symptoms after repair
- Recurrence rates <5% with mesh repair
- Resolution of outlet obstruction symptoms in 60-80% of cases
- Mortality rare, primarily associated with strangulation or sepsis in elderly patients

## Patient Education

- Postoperative expectations:
  - Improved urinary flow and emptying
  - Resolution of two-stage voiding
  - Return to normal activities within 4-6 weeks
- Warning signs requiring immediate evaluation:
  - Acute urinary retention
  - Significant hematuria
  - Fever or wound drainage
  - Recurrent swelling
