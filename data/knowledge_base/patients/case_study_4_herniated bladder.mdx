# Case Study: 74-Year-Old Male with Inguinal Swelling and Lower Urinary Tract Symptoms

## Patient Information

**Demographics:**
- 74-year-old male
- Retired mechanic
- Widower, lives alone

**Chief Complaint:**
- Progressive right groin swelling and difficulty with urination

## History of Present Illness

The patient presents with a 9-month history of gradually enlarging, painless swelling in the right groin, which has recently become more pronounced and occasionally extends into the scrotum. He reports a sensation of heaviness and fullness in the groin, particularly after standing for long periods. Over the past 2 months, he has noticed difficulty initiating urination, a weak urinary stream, and increased urinary frequency, especially at night. He describes a peculiar need to compress the groin swelling to fully empty his bladder, with a noticeable decrease in the size of the swelling after voiding. He denies hematuria, fever, chills, or dysuria. No history of trauma, prior hernia repair, or abdominal surgery.

## Past Medical History

- Hypertension (well controlled)
- Benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH), on medical therapy for 3 years
- Type 2 diabetes mellitus, controlled with oral agents
- No prior abdominal or pelvic surgeries

## Medications

- Tamsulosin 0.4 mg daily
- Metformin 500 mg twice daily
- Lisinopril 10 mg daily
- Aspirin 81 mg daily

## Allergies

- No known drug allergies

## Family History

- Father had prostate cancer at age 78
- Mother had type 2 diabetes
- No family history of hernias

## Social History

- Former smoker (quit 10 years ago, 20 pack-year history)
- Alcohol: 1-2 drinks per week
- Diet: Mixed, moderate physical activity
- Lives alone, independent in activities of daily living

## Review of Systems

- **Constitutional:** No fever, weight loss, or night sweats
- **Genitourinary:** Increased frequency, nocturia, weak stream, incomplete emptying, no hematuria
- **Gastrointestinal:** No constipation, nausea, or vomiting
- **Musculoskeletal:** No pain or limitation of movement
- **Other systems:** Negative

**General:** Elderly male, alert, in no acute distress

**Abdominal:** Soft, non-tender, no palpable masses, no organomegaly

**Genitourinary:**
- Right groin: Large, non-tender, soft swelling extending into the right hemiscrotum. Swelling is partially reducible and decreases in size after voiding. No overlying skin changes.
- Left groin: No swelling
- Testes: Normal, non-tender, no masses

**Rectal Examination:**
- Prostate: Moderately enlarged, smooth, non-tender
- Stool: Hemoccult negative

**Other systems:** Unremarkable

## Laboratory Results

**Renal Function:**

**Urinalysis:**
- Clear, no blood, no protein, no leukocytes

**Fasting Glucose:** 122 mg/dL

## Diagnostic Studies

**Ultrasound of Groin/Scrotum:**
- Large, fluid-filled structure in the right inguinal canal and scrotum, continuous with the urinary bladder. The structure decreases in size after voiding.
- No evidence of bowel or omentum in the hernia sac.
- Both testes normal.

**Retrograde Cystography:**
- Wide-mouthed, rounded protrusion of the bladder wall extending into the right inguinal canal.
- Herniated portion fills with contrast during voiding phase, confirming communication with the bladder.

**Non-contrast Pelvic CT:**
- Bladder base angulated anteroinferiorly toward the right inguinal canal.
- Herniation of a significant portion of the bladder into the right inguinal canal and scrotum.
- No evidence of bowel incarceration or strangulation.
- No hydronephrosis or upper tract dilation.

**Post-void Imaging:**
- Marked reduction in hernia sac size after complete bladder emptying.

## Assessment and Plan

**Assessment:**
74-year-old male with classic clinical and imaging features of right inguinoscrotal bladder hernia (paraperitoneal type), presenting with lower urinary tract symptoms and characteristic post-void reduction in hernia size. No evidence of bowel involvement or strangulation. Background of BPH and well-controlled diabetes.

**Plan:**

1. **Surgical Management:**
   - Elective open hernia repair with reduction of herniated bladder and reinforcement of the inguinal canal (Lichtenstein mesh technique recommended, unless contraindicated by intraoperative findings)[4][7][8].
   - Intraoperative cystoscopic guidance to minimize risk of bladder injury during dissection[7].
   - Bladder wall inspected for ischemia or injury; repair performed if necessary.

2. **Perioperative Management:**
   - Foley catheter placement preoperatively for bladder decompression and intraoperative monitoring.
   - Perioperative antibiotics as per protocol.
   - Monitor urine output and signs of hematuria postoperatively.

3. **BPH Management:**
   - Continue tamsulosin perioperatively.
   - Reassess lower urinary tract symptoms after hernia repair; many patients experience improvement[4][7].

4. **Postoperative Care:**
   - Early ambulation, pain control, and wound care.
   - Foley catheter typically removed within 48-72 hours postoperatively, depending on intraoperative findings and urinary output.
   - Monitor for complications: urinary retention, infection, or hernia recurrence.

5. **Follow-Up:**
   - Outpatient follow-up in 2 weeks for wound check and assessment of urinary function.
   - Consider urology referral if persistent or worsening lower urinary tract symptoms after hernia repair.

**Patient Education:**
- Explained the rare nature of bladder herniation as a cause of inguinal swelling and urinary symptoms.
- Discussed the surgical approach, risks (including bladder injury, infection, recurrence), and expected recovery.
- Reviewed the importance of reporting any new symptoms such as hematuria, fever, or inability to void.

**Teaching Points:**
- Bladder hernias are rare (1-3% of inguinal hernias), most often seen in older, obese males with lower urinary tract symptoms and/or BPH[4][6][8].
- Classic symptoms include two-stage urination (manual compression of hernia to complete voiding) and reduction in hernia size after voiding[4][6].
- Diagnosis is best confirmed with retrograde cystography or CT, which delineate the herniated bladder and guide surgical planning[5][6][8].
- Surgical repair with mesh is the standard of care; intraoperative cystoscopy may reduce the risk of bladder injury[7][8].
- Most patients experience significant improvement in urinary symptoms after successful hernia repair[4][7].
